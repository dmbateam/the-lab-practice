    How the lab works

    Test cheap. Build only what the data backs.

    Every experiment is one loop:

    1. Pick a problem — why isn't activation higher right now?
    2. Pick a solution — a candidate fix for that problem.
    3. Choose a test — cheap and noisy, or pricey and reliable?
    4. Read the data — the test says accept or reject, with an estimate.
    5. Build or drop — building spends the build cost and moves the metric for real.

    Repeat until you run out of budget or weeks, or you hit the target. Your whole history is your Progress Sheet.

    Two things to remember
    • 💸 One shared budget. Tests and builds both spend your $50K. Screen ideas cheaply first — then spend big only on the winners.
    • 🛡️ Activation is your score. Retention is the real goal. Some ideas lift activation but secretly hurt retention. Don't break the guardrail.
    How you win
    • 🏆 Best value wins. Your score is activation gained per dollar — a cheap win beats an expensive one.
    • 🎯 45% is the goal, but getting part-way for far less money can still put you top.
    • 💡 Leftover budget is fine; wasted budget isn't.

    The lesson

    The smallest valid test that moves the metric beats the biggest build.

    • The cheapest valid test beats the biggest build. A team that screened cheaply and built only the winners gets more activation per dollar than one that full-built everything. Efficiency is the score, not raw lift.
    • Activation is the score; retention is the real goal. The board ranks you on activation per dollar — but the guardrail (week-4 retention) is what actually keeps Cadence alive. A win that breaks it isn't a win.
    • Name the trap: the sample-workspace. Auto-filling new accounts with fake check-ins was the cheapest way to spike activation (+11) — and it quietly cost 8 points of retention. The primary metric went up while the real goal fell. That's the Metrics Canvas lesson in product form. Only a strong test (an A/B on a live cohort) could catch it before you shipped.
    • The durable win was “invite a teammate first.” It fixed the root cause — a check-in is worthless until a teammate sees it — lifting both activation and retention.
